My MDAP’s Internship Story

My project

  • GenAI use for Mental Health Support Issue
    • in collaboration with LGBTIQ+ Health Australia
  • Dashboard for Coroners Court Victoria
    • in collaboration with Coronus Court Victoria & Engineering Student link

On the surface, these two projects could not be more different — one concerned with the potential harms of AI, the other with its operational benefits. Yet together they posed a single, urgent question that has come to define my internship experience: who does technology serve, and at what cost to whom? This essay reflects on that question through the lens of my own evolving professional identity, the value I created and received, and the ways in which this experience has reshaped my sense of purpose as I approach the final stages of my Masters degree.

Challenges

Beyond the disciplinary gap, the internship also demanded a fundamental shift in how I approached work itself. As a student, I was accustomed to theoretical frameworks and the luxury of deliberation. At MDAP, I was operating in a live research environment with real deadlines and real communities at stake. This transition from student to practitioner was uncomfortable at times, but deeply formative. It taught me that professional expertise is not simply knowing more; it is the confidence to apply what you know, even in unfamiliar terrain.

Learnings

During my internship at MDAP, the highlight of my projects are the two sides of the impact of technology. My project on AI and mental health showcases the harm of AI use without sufficient preparation and regulations. And this is happening right now around us! On the other hand, the Coronus Court project showcases how the technology can help human in so many ways including make job more efficient, reduce the trauma and bias.

I began this internship as a tech optimist. I end it as something more considered, a critical advocate for technology that is genuinely equitable and designed in genuine partnership with the communities it affects. The two projects I worked on at MDAP did not produce the same conclusions, and that is precisely the point. Technology is not inherently beneficial or harmful; it is shaped by the values, structures, and decisions of the people who build and deploy it.
