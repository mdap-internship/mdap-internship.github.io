Project VicExtinct

  • Supervised by Damien Mannon in collaboration with Lynette Loke

Overview

Context

Marine biodiversity is known to be changing rapidly, but there is no clear integrated baseline of which species occurs and their location along the coast. This lack of a baseline makes it hard to quantify change, anticipate future declines, and prioritise conservation efforts.

Challenge

Biodiversity records are scattered across many repositories with different formats and detail, making them technically difficult to extract and combine at scale. This fragmentation creates a critical bottleneck, preventing the creation of an accessible, standardised dataset needed for extinction modelling.

Aim

The project aims to model marine species extinctions and declines along Victoria’s coasts, identifying what has been lost, which species are most at risk, and where to prioritise conservation efforts. MDAP’s role is to build a reproducible, automated workflow that consolidates all relevant records into the first unified dataset suitable for extinction modelling and mapping extinction risk.

Solution

To build an automated and reproducible workflow leveraging the Snakemake workflow manager to extract, standardise, clean, and integrate occurrence records into analysis-ready dataset for extinction modelling.

My Scope

My scope was to incorporate two repositories into the workflow. They are:

Ocean Biodiversity Information System (OBIS)

The Ocean Biodiversity Information System (OBIS) is a global data system that focuses specifically on marine and coastal biodiversity, providing open access to information on the distribution and diversity of ocean life. It aggregates marine species‑occurrence data (e.g. surveys, monitoring, historical records, and increasingly DNA‑derived records).

Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF)

The Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) is an international network and data infrastructure funded by governments to provide anyone, anywhere, open access to data about all types of life on Earth. GBIF’s data sources range from museum specimens and herbarium sheets to modern observations, DNA barcodes and citizen‑science records.

Research Outcomes

Through this project, I experienced the end‑to‑end lifecycle of building a workflow, from initial requirements gathering through to a first iterative pipeline deliverable. I contributed by broadening the range of data sources used in the workflow to include OBIS and GBIF alongside existing repositories.

Workflow:

The workflow begins with data acquisition from multiple biodiversity repositories, such as the Atlas of Living Australia (ALA), OBIS, GBIF, and related sources. The extracted data is then pre‑processed into a standardised table with a common set of columns, including species, latitude, longitude, and year.

After preparation, the data passes through a series of shared downstream steps, such as normalising taxonomic names and filtering by coastal distance. Up to and including the construction of the source matrix, these downstream steps are executed independently for each data source using Snakemake.

Finally, the processed data from each source is transformed into a source matrix and then aggregated into a single occurrence matrix.



Sample Workflow Output:

The output of the workflow is an occurrence matrix showing the presence of different species over time. Each square in the matrix indicates whether a particular species was observed in a given year, with blue representing presence and white representing no recorded observation. This visualisation helps identify species that may warrant further investigation for potential extinction risk.

Project ESCAPE

  • Supervised by Kabir Manandhar Shrestha (and special mention Robert Turnbull) in collaboration with Sam Wilks

Overview

Context

Viruses like influenza are constantly evolving, accumulating many genetic changes that help them escape existing population immunity and reduce vaccine effectiveness. Global surveillance can only experimentally test a small fraction of circulating strains so computational models such as machine learning models are increasingly used to predict “immune escape” from genetic sequence data.

Challenge

Existing models developed by our external collaborators work well mainly by interpolating between mutations that have already been seen in the training data. However, they perform substantially worse in predicting a small critical set of new genetic changes that lead to immune escape which is exactly what matters most for early detection of variants of concern and for vaccine updates.

Aims

The overall aim is to develop and implement models that reliably detect when genetic changes not seen in the training data are likely to cause immune escape, using historical and recent influenza surveillance data as primary test cases.

A sub-aim that was worked on was to develop and validate modelling approaches that incorporate sequence-structure representations to improve detection of antigenic effects caused by genetic mutation.

My Scope

My scope focused on visualising and interpreting differences in viral protein sequence representations generated by the Evolutionary Scale Model (ESM) family of language models. In particular, I analysed ESM‑based embeddings of multiple labelled groups of viral protein sequences to explore how well these models separate biologically meaningful virus clusters in a low‑dimensional space.

Research Outcomes

Through this project, I gained hands‑on experience applying machine learning to a familiar domain in infection and immunity. I investigated differences in ESM embeddings for several viral protein groups using dimensionality reduction techniques, primarily principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). I also compared how embeddings from different ESM2 models behaved under the same dimensionality reduction pipeline, highlighting similarities and discrepancies in how each model captures viral sequence diversity.

UMAP of Viral Protein Sequence Embeddings (ESM2_650M)

I projected viral protein embeddings from the ESM2_650M model into a two‑dimensional UMAP space to qualitatively assess the separation of different virus groups. Viral protein sequences from distinct virus clusters were embedded using ESM2_650M and then mapped into a fixed UMAP layout, allowing direct visual comparison of how new groups integrate into the existing representation.
